    How it works

    What it measures

    After a sharp sound, a room keeps it bouncing between walls, floor and ceiling. The echo time (acousticians call it RT60) is how long that takes to fade by 60 decibels, to about a millionth of its energy. Bare rooms with hard walls ring for a second or more; furnished rooms settle in under half a second.

    How one clap is enough

    Clapback records the quiet moment before your clap (to learn the room’s background noise) and three seconds after it. It splits the sound into octave bands, subtracts the noise, adds up the remaining energy backwards in time (Schroeder’s method), and fits a straight line to the decay. The echo time shown is the average of the 500 Hz, 1 kHz and 2 kHz bands, where speech lives.

    How accurate it is

    On synthetic rooms with known echo times, the average error is 2–4 %, and 90 % of readings are within 9 % even when the clap is only 35 dB above the room noise. Real rooms are messier, so expect about ±10–20 %: plenty to tell dry from echoey and to see a change work. It is not a certified measurement. Full accuracy report.

    Tips for a good reading

    • One sharp clap with cupped hands, 1–2 m from the phone or laptop.
    • Stay silent for three seconds after it.
    • Clap three times; Clapback shows the middle value.
    • Turn off fans and close the door if you can.
